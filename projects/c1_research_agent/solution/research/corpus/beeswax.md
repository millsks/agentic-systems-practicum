# Beeswax and Comb

Beeswax is made by the bees themselves. Young workers, typically between about twelve and eighteen days old, secrete
small transparent wax flakes from eight glands on the underside of the abdomen. They chew the flakes to soften them and
shape them into comb.

Comb is built from six-sided cells arranged back to back in vertical sheets. The hexagon is an efficient shape: it
tiles a surface with no gaps and encloses the most storage area for the least wax. Worker cells, drone cells, and queen
cells differ in size, and the same cells are reused for brood, pollen, and honey over many seasons.

Wax is expensive for a colony. Bees must eat several kilograms of honey to produce one kilogram of wax, and wax-making
needs a warm hive. That cost is one reason beekeepers return empty comb to colonies after harvesting honey. Humans have
used beeswax for thousands of years, for candles, waterproofing, polishes, and casting metal.
