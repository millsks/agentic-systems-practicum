# How Bees Make Honey

Honey starts as flower nectar, a watery sugar solution that is mostly sucrose. A forager sucks nectar into her honey
stomach (crop), where enzymes begin to work on it, and carries it back to the hive. There she passes it to house bees,
who add more enzymes, notably invertase, which splits sucrose into the simpler sugars glucose and fructose.

Fresh nectar can be well over half water, which would ferment in storage. House bees spread it thinly in cells and fan
their wings to drive air through the hive, evaporating water until the honey is only about seventeen to eighteen
percent water. At that concentration yeasts and bacteria cannot grow. The bees then cap each full cell with a thin
layer of beeswax.

Honey is the colony's winter fuel. A strong colony can store tens of kilograms of it, far more than it needs, which is
why beekeepers can harvest the surplus. The color and flavor of a honey depend on the flowers the nectar came from.
