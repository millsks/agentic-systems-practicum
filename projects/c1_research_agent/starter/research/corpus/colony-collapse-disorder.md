# Colony Collapse Disorder

Colony collapse disorder (CCD) is the name given to a pattern of sudden colony loss reported by beekeepers in the
United States beginning in the winter of 2006 to 2007. In a classic case, most adult workers vanish from an
apparently healthy hive, leaving behind the queen, capped brood, plenty of food, and few or no dead bees.

No single cause has been identified. Researchers have pointed to a combination of stressors acting together: varroa
mites and the viruses they spread, other pathogens such as Nosema, exposure to pesticides including neonicotinoids,
poor nutrition where bees forage on large single-crop landscapes, and the stress of moving colonies long distances for
pollination.

Reports of the classic CCD pattern became much less common in later years, but high overall rates of colony loss
continue, especially over winter. Beekeepers replace lost colonies by splitting strong ones and buying new queens,
which is costly. For a closer look at the mite, see the document on varroa.
