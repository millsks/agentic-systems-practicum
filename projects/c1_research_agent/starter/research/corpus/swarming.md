# Swarming and Choosing a New Home

Swarming is how a honeybee colony reproduces. In spring, a crowded colony raises new queens. Before they emerge, the
old queen leaves with a large part of the workforce, often around half or more of the workers, and they settle as a
cluster on a branch nearby. The bees left behind stay with the brood, stores, and a new queen.

While the swarm hangs in its cluster, a few hundred experienced scout bees search for a cavity to live in, judging
volume, entrance size, and height. Scouts return and advertise promising sites with waggle dances. Better sites earn
longer, more enthusiastic dances, so more scouts go to inspect them. When enough scouts are present at one site at the
same time, a quorum is reached, and the swarm takes off and flies there together.

The biologist Thomas Seeley described this process in his book Honeybee Democracy as a model of group decision-making.
Beekeepers try to prevent swarming by giving colonies more space, because a swarm takes much of the honey crop with it.
