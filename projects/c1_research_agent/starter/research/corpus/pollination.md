# Honeybees as Pollinators

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the male parts of a flower to the female parts, which lets the plant set
seed and fruit. Honeybees are not the only pollinators, and wild bees are more effective for some crops, but honeybees
are uniquely useful to agriculture because colonies are large and can be moved to wherever a crop is blooming.

Many fruit, nut, and seed crops benefit from bee pollination, including apples, cherries, blueberries, and melons.
California's almond orchards are the largest single example: each spring a very large share of the commercial
honeybee colonies in the United States are trucked there to pollinate the bloom, and growers pay beekeepers rental
fees for every hive.

A useful trait of honeybees is flower constancy: on a given foraging trip a bee tends to visit flowers of only one
species, which means the pollen she carries usually reaches the right kind of flower. Staple grains such as wheat,
rice, and corn are wind-pollinated and do not need bees.
